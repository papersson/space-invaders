# Find, Rank, Measure

Status: revised after round 2 (the first passing round), before review round 3, the final round

## Argument

**Audience.** A relatively technical person new to search, like an undergraduate taking a first course in information retrieval. They can read a formula and a bit of code, and know what a vector and a logarithm are, but know nothing about search internals: no inverted index, no BM25, no evaluation metrics.

**Question.** Type two words, "coronavirus origin", into a search box over 171,332 scientific papers, and ten titles come back, best first, in about two milliseconds. How? It is two problems: finding the papers that match without reading them all, and putting the best ones first. A third makes it engineering: when you change something, how do you know the results got better?

**Answer.** The work happens before anyone searches: an analyzer turns every paper into terms, and an inverted index keeps, for every term, the posting list of papers that contain it. A query reads only its own terms' posting lists (5,482 and 35,095 entries here), not every word of every paper: two milliseconds, against a tenth of a second to scan this collection, and minutes at web scale. BM25 ranks the matches: TF-IDF with two fixes, saturation (k1) and length normalization (b). Dense retrieval with an embedding model catches the same meaning in other words, approximate nearest-neighbour search keeps it fast, hybrid search covers dense retrieval's blind spot for exact codes by merging both rankings, and a slow, accurate cross-encoder reranks only the top hundred: a funnel. A test collection (papers, topics, pooled human relevance judgments) with metrics such as precision@k, recall@k, MRR and NDCG says in numbers whether each stage helped: on the fifty topics, NDCG@10 goes from 0.30 (TF-IDF) to 0.61 (BM25), 0.68 (hybrid) and 0.74 (reranked). Online A/B tests then check a change with real users, whose clicks are informative but biased.

**Takeaway.** Search is an index that finds candidates without reading everything, rankers that order them in stages from cheap to expensive, and an evaluation loop (relevance judgments and metrics, then online tests) that tells you whether a change helped.

**Wrong model.** A search engine looks through the documents for your words when you search, the best results are the ones that use your words most often, and whether results got better is obvious when you look at them. (Canonical misconceptions from both research passes: "search is just keyword matching", "more matches is better", "embeddings replaced keyword search", "clicks measure relevance".)

**Objectives.**
1. Explain how an analyzer and an inverted index find matching papers without reading the collection, and why the query must go through the same analyzer as the index.
2. Explain BM25 as TF-IDF plus term-frequency saturation (k1) and length normalization (b), and say what dense retrieval, approximate nearest-neighbour search, hybrid fusion and cross-encoder reranking each add, in a retrieve-then-rerank funnel.
3. Define a test collection with pooled relevance judgments, precision and recall and their trade-off, precision@k, recall@k, MRR and NDCG, and compute NDCG@10 for a short ranked list.
4. Read an offline comparison of pipeline stages, and say why online evaluation (A/B tests) is still needed and why clicks are a biased signal.

## Chain

1. The question: two words, 171,332 papers, ten titles in two milliseconds. How? Reading every paper per query takes a tenth of a second here, and minutes at web scale. Therefore the work must happen before anyone searches.
2. Therefore build an inverted index: for every word, the posting list of papers that contain it. A query reads only its words' lists, intersecting or merging them: forty thousand entries, not every word of every paper.
3. But which strings are the same word? Therefore an analyzer (tokenize, lowercase, stop words, stem) turns text into terms, the same way at index time and query time; analyze them differently and results vanish (the capitalized lookup finds zero papers).
4. But the index only says which of 38,933 papers match, not which ten are best. Therefore rank: TF-IDF puts a paper about DNA replication origins first and a very long paper second; therefore BM25 adds saturation (k1) and length normalization (b), and the top paper is about the virus's origin.
5. But BM25 only counts words that match: for "how long does coronavirus survive on surfaces", the relevant papers say persistence and stability. Therefore dense retrieval with a bi-encoder and cosine similarity (ten of ten relevant); but comparing every vector is a scan again, therefore approximate nearest-neighbour search (HNSW).
6. But dense retrieval blurs exact codes (D614G), therefore hybrid search merges both rankings with reciprocal rank fusion. But both score the query and each paper separately; a cross-encoder reads them together and ranks better, but is far too slow for the whole collection. Therefore a funnel: cheap retrieval picks candidates, the reranker orders the top hundred, each stage with its own job.
7. But how do you know any of these steps helped? Looking won't tell you (two plausible top tens for coronavirus origin). Therefore a test collection: papers, topics and relevance judgments, built by pooling because judging everything is impossible (and unjudged papers count as not relevant).
8. Therefore precision and recall; but they trade off, and a ranked list is read from the top, therefore measure at a cutoff: precision@10 for what people see, recall@100 for what the reranker gets.
9. But precision@10 ignores order and grades. Therefore MRR (the first relevant result) and NDCG (graded gains, discounted by rank, normalized by the ideal ranking), worked on the opening's own top ten.
10. Therefore measure every stage on all fifty topics: each stage adds on average, though a stage can hurt single topics (the reranker on coronavirus origin), and the table shows what each metric can and cannot see.
11. But offline judgments are not users, therefore online A/B tests; but clicks are biased and offline wins don't always carry over, therefore teams use both.
12. Therefore the answer: index, staged rankers and the evaluation loop, calling back to the opening's two milliseconds and ten papers; one line on retrieval-augmented generation.

Deviations from the canonical progression: both research reports give the textbook order (index and Boolean retrieval, text analysis, TF-IDF, BM25, evaluation), with the modern additions (dense retrieval, ANN, hybrid, reranking) after BM25; the script follows it, and puts evaluation after the whole pipeline so that its metrics can score every stage the viewer has seen. The textbooks show Boolean retrieval as the simpler version before ranking; here Boolean AND and OR appear only as the two ways of reading posting lists, since the approved outline goes straight to ranking. The vector space model and cosine-normalized TF-IDF are skipped (TF-IDF appears only as the unnormalized sum that BM25 fixes); MAP, interpolated precision and F1 are left out, as both reports suggest for a short lesson. The textbooks' worked examples (Shakespeare, the car/insurance table) are replaced by one real public test collection, TREC-COVID, so that every number in the index, ranking and evaluation chapters comes from the same papers and the same fifty topics. The worked examples use the topics' short keyword queries (what a person types); the results table uses the topics' questions, the standard benchmark setup, and says so; the keyword-query table is in the evidence (the neural stages gain less there).

### 2. The inverted index

> Before any search, go through every paper once and flip it around: for every word, keep a list of the papers that contain it. That's an inverted index.
> The list for one word is its posting list. Each entry, a posting, holds a paper's ID, how many times the word appears in it, and where, so a search can also ask for an exact phrase.
> In this collection, the list for origin has 5,482 papers. The list for coronavirus, in the form the index stores it, has 35,095.
> A query reads only the lists for its own words. The lists are sorted by paper ID, so you can walk two of them side by side and keep the papers on both: 1,644 have both words. Or merge them, for papers with either word: 38,933.
> Either way, the work is in those two lists, about forty thousand postings, not every word of 171 thousand papers. That's how the index answers in two milliseconds.

### 3. Text analysis

> But which strings count as the same word? Origin, origins, Origin with a capital O?
> Before anything goes into the index, an analyzer turns text into terms. Here's the title of that top paper. The analyzer splits it into tokens, and lowercases them. An English analyzer, like this one, also drops stop words, very common words like of and is. And it stems: it cuts word endings off by rule, so origins becomes origin. The stems don't have to be real words: coronavirus becomes coronaviru.
> The query goes through the same analyzer, so its terms land on the same posting lists.
> Skip that step, and results vanish. Look up Coronavirus exactly as typed, without the analyzer, and the index has no such term: zero papers. Lowercasing alone isn't enough either: the index stores the stem. Analyzing the index one way and the query another is a classic search bug.

### 4. Ranking

> Now 38,933 papers contain at least one of the words. Which ten go first?
> A classic answer is TF-IDF, in its simplest form. Term frequency: a paper that uses a term more is probably more about it. Inverse document frequency: a rare term tells you more than a common one. Origin is in about one paper in thirty-one, so its weight is the natural log of thirty-one: about 3.4. Coronavirus is in about one in five, so it weighs 1.6. A paper's score is each term's count times its weight, added up.
> Rank by that, and the top paper is about DNA replication origins. It says origin fourteen times, and never mentions a coronavirus. Second is a paper twenty-five times the average length: long enough to say coronavirus twenty-one times.
> BM25 fixes both problems. First, saturation: each extra mention adds less than the one before. A parameter called k1 sets how fast the credit levels off; its default is 1.2. Second, length normalization: a longer paper needs more mentions for the same credit. A parameter called b sets how much length counts; its default is 0.75.
> With BM25, the top paper is about the origins of SARS-CoV-2, the virus behind COVID-19. It uses each word three times, in a shorter than average paper.
> BM25 came out of a probabilistic model of relevance in the 1990s, and it's still the default ranking in Lucene, the search library inside Elasticsearch.

### 5. Meaning, not just words

> But BM25 only counts words that match. Take another topic in this collection: how long does coronavirus survive on surfaces. Four of BM25's top ten are relevant; one of the others is about the surface of the eye. The relevant papers mostly say persistence, or stability, not survive. That's the vocabulary mismatch problem: the same meaning, in different words.
> Dense retrieval matches meaning instead. An embedding model turns a text into a vector: here, a list of 384 numbers. It's a neural network, trained on pairs of related texts, so that texts with similar meanings get vectors that point in similar directions. It encodes the query and each paper separately, so it's called a bi-encoder, and every paper's vector is computed once, in advance. Papers are ranked by cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between their vector and the query's. On the surfaces question, all ten of its top results are relevant.
> But comparing the query with 171 thousand vectors is a scan again. So vector search uses approximate nearest-neighbour search. A common method is HNSW. It links each vector to a few of its nearest neighbours, in layers of graphs, and a search hops from neighbour to neighbour toward the query. Here it compared about twelve hundred vectors, not 171 thousand, and still found about ninety-nine percent of the true top ten. Approximate means it can miss a few.

### 6. Hybrid, then rerank

> Dense retrieval has its own blind spot: exact names and codes. Search for D614G, the name of a mutation in the virus. All of BM25's top ten contain it. Only one of dense retrieval's does; most of the rest are conference abstract lists, with codes like P441.
> So run both, and merge the two rankings. That's hybrid search. Their scores aren't on the same scale, so merge by rank. Reciprocal rank fusion takes a paper's rank in each ranking, adds a constant, usually sixty, and takes one over that. Then it adds the two up.
> Both still score the query and each paper separately. A cross-encoder reads them together, as one input, so every word of the query can be weighed against every word of the paper. It usually ranks better, but it's slow: on this computer, about thirty-six papers a second. The whole collection would take over an hour, for one query.
> So search is built as a funnel. Cheap retrieval over the whole collection picks the candidates, in milliseconds. The expensive reranker orders only the top hundred. A separate model can also learn to combine that score with other signals, like freshness and past clicks: that's learning to rank.
> So each stage has its own job. Retrieval must not lose the good papers. Reranking must put the best of them on top.

### 7. Relevance judgments

> Every step so far was supposed to make the results better. How do you check? Here are two top tens for coronavirus origin: BM25's, and the full pipeline's, with the reranker on top. Both read as plausible. Looking won't tell you which is better.
> You need to know which papers are actually relevant, and that takes people. A test collection has three parts: the papers, a set of topics, each a query with a description of what the searcher wants, and relevance judgments, often called qrels.
> This collection comes from TREC-COVID, a 2020 evaluation run by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. Judging every paper for each of fifty topics would take over eight and a half million judgments. So TREC uses pooling. Dozens of teams submitted rankings. The top papers from those rankings were pooled. Then medical students and National Library of Medicine indexers judged each pooled paper: relevant, partially relevant, or not relevant. About sixty-six thousand judgments in all.
> A paper nobody judged counts as not relevant. That's pooling's known weakness: a new system that finds different papers can be scored too low.

### 8. Precision and recall

> With judgments, you can score any list of results. Precision: of the papers returned, the fraction that are relevant. BM25's top ten has nine relevant papers: precision 0.9. The reranked list has four: 0.4. Recall: of all the relevant papers, the fraction that were returned.
> They pull against each other. For coronavirus origin, 637 papers are judged relevant. BM25's nine are a recall of only 1.4 percent. Its top thousand finds 255: recall 40 percent, but precision down to about a quarter. Return more, and recall can only rise, while precision usually falls.
> A ranked list is read from the top, so measure it at a cutoff. Precision at ten counts the relevant papers in the top ten: what people actually see. Recall at a hundred asks what share of all the relevant papers made the top hundred: what a reranker gets to see.

### 9. Order and grades

> Precision at ten ignores order: a relevant paper counts the same in first place as in tenth. Two measures care about order.
> Reciprocal rank is one over the rank of the first relevant result: one if it's first, a half if it's second. Its average over all the topics is the mean reciprocal rank, MRR. It suits searches with one right answer.
> NDCG also uses the grades. Each paper's gain is its grade: two for relevant, one for partially relevant. Divide each gain by a discount that grows with the rank. To get the discount, add one to the rank, and take the log, base two. Then add them all up. That's the discounted cumulative gain, or DCG. For BM25's top ten, it's 7.06.
> The best possible top ten, ten papers graded two, would score 9.09. Divide one by the other, and you get normalized DCG, NDCG at ten: 0.78, where one is perfect.

### 11. Offline and online

> Those are offline numbers: fixed judgments, repeatable, and cheap to rerun after every change. But judges aren't your users. Online evaluation shows a change to a random share of real users, an A/B test, and compares what they do: what they click, how often they rephrase, how often they give up.
> Clicks are biased, though. In one eye-tracking study, when the top two results were swapped, people still clicked the first one more, even when the second was more relevant.
> And offline wins don't always carry over. In another study, systems that scored better on judged results didn't help real users do their search tasks any better.
> So teams use both: offline tests to choose what's worth trying, online tests to decide what ships.

### 12. The answer

> So how do ten good papers come back from 171 thousand, in two milliseconds?
> Before any search, an analyzer turns every paper into terms, and an inverted index lists the papers for each term. A query reads a few posting lists, never the whole collection, and BM25 ranks the matches. To do better, add stages: vectors for the same meaning in other words, and a reranker for the top hundred, each costing more time.
> And whatever you change, relevance judgments and measures like NDCG tell you, in numbers, whether it helped. Nine of that first top ten were relevant.
> That top ten matters more than ever. Retrieval-augmented generation searches first, and puts the top results into a language model's context. The model's answer can only be as good as what search found.
