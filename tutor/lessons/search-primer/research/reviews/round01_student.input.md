### 1. The question

> Type two words into a search box: coronavirus origin. Behind it are 171,332 scientific papers. Back come ten titles, best first, in about two milliseconds.
> How? It's two problems. Find the papers that match, without reading them all. Then put the best ones first.
> And a third problem makes it engineering. When you change something, how do you know the results got better?
> The obvious way is to read every paper, every time. On this computer, just checking all 171 thousand papers for the two words takes about a tenth of a second. But a web search engine covers hundreds of billions of pages. At this speed, even a billion papers like these would take over ten minutes, for one query.
> So the work has to happen before anyone searches.

### 2. The inverted index

> Before any search, go through every paper once and flip it around: for every word, keep a list of the papers that contain it. That's an inverted index.
> The list for one word is its posting list. Each entry, a posting, holds a paper's ID, how many times the word appears in it, and where.
> In this collection, the list for origin has 5,482 papers. The list for coronavirus has 35,095.
> A query reads only the lists for its own words. The lists are sorted by paper ID, so you can walk two of them side by side and keep the papers on both: 1,644 have both words. Or merge them, for papers with either word: 38,933.
> Either way, the work is reading those two lists, about forty thousand entries, not every word of 171 thousand papers. That's how the index answers in two milliseconds.

### 3. Text analysis

### 4. Ranking

> Now 38,933 papers contain at least one of the words. Which ten go first?
> A classic answer is TF-IDF. Term frequency: a paper that uses a word more is probably more about it. Inverse document frequency: a rare word tells you more than a common one. Origin is in about one paper in thirty-one, so its weight is the natural log of thirty-one: about 3.4. Coronavirus is in about one in five, so it weighs 1.6. A paper's score is each word's count times its weight, added up.
> Rank by that, and the top paper is about DNA replication origins. It says origin fourteen times, and never mentions a coronavirus. Second is a paper twenty-five times the average length.
> BM25 fixes both problems. First, saturation: each extra mention adds less than the one before. A parameter called k1 sets how fast the credit levels off; its default is 1.2. Second, length normalization: a longer paper needs more mentions for the same credit. A parameter called b sets how much length counts; its default is 0.75.
> With BM25, the top paper is about the origins of SARS-CoV-2, the virus behind COVID-19. It uses each word three times, in a shorter than average paper.
> BM25 comes from the 1990s, and it's still the default ranking in Lucene, the search library inside Elasticsearch.

### 5. Meaning, not just words

> But BM25 only counts words that match. Take another topic in this collection: how long does coronavirus survive on surfaces. Four of BM25's top ten are relevant; one of the others is about the surface of the eye. The relevant papers mostly say persistence, or stability, not survive. That's the vocabulary mismatch problem: the same meaning, in different words.
> Dense retrieval matches meaning instead. An embedding model, a neural network trained on pairs of related texts, turns a text into a vector, here 384 numbers, so that texts with similar meanings point in similar directions. It encodes the query and each paper separately, so it's called a bi-encoder, and every paper's vector is computed once, in advance. Papers are ranked by cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between their vector and the query's. On the surfaces question, all ten of its top results are relevant.
> But comparing the query with 171 thousand vectors is a scan again. So vector search uses approximate nearest-neighbour search. A common method, HNSW, links each vector to a few near neighbours, in layers of graphs, and hops greedily toward the query. Here it compared about twelve hundred vectors, not 171 thousand, and still found about ninety-nine percent of the true top ten. Approximate means it can miss a few.

### 6. Hybrid, then rerank

> Dense retrieval has its own blind spot: exact names and codes. Search for D614G, the name of a mutation in the virus. All of BM25's top ten contain it. Only one of dense retrieval's does; most of the rest are conference abstract lists, with codes like P441.
> So run both, and merge the two rankings. That's hybrid search. Their scores aren't on the same scale, so merge by rank. Reciprocal rank fusion gives a paper one over sixty plus its rank, from each ranking, and adds them up.
> Both still score the query and each paper separately. A cross-encoder reads them together, as one input, so every word of the query can be weighed against every word of the paper. It ranks better, but it's slow: on this computer, about thirty-five papers a second. The whole collection would take over an hour, for one query.
> So search is built as a funnel. Cheap retrieval over the whole collection picks the candidates, in milliseconds. The expensive reranker orders only the top hundred. Rerankers can also learn to weigh other signals, like freshness and past clicks: that's learning to rank.
> So each stage has its own job. Retrieval must not lose the good papers. Reranking must put the best of them on top.

### 7. Relevance judgments

> Every step so far was supposed to make the results better. How do you check?
> You need to know which papers are actually relevant, and that takes people. A test collection has three parts: the papers, a set of topics, each a query with a description of what the searcher wants, and relevance judgments, often called qrels.
> This collection comes from TREC-COVID, a 2020 evaluation run by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. Judging every paper for each of fifty topics would take eight and a half million judgments. So TREC uses pooling. Dozens of teams submitted rankings, the top papers from those rankings were pooled, and people with medical expertise judged each one: relevant, partially relevant, or not relevant. About sixty-six thousand judgments in all.
> A paper nobody judged counts as not relevant. That's pooling's known weakness: a new system that finds different papers can be scored too low.

### 8. Precision and recall

> With judgments, you can score any list of results. Precision: of the papers returned, the fraction that are relevant. Recall: of all the relevant papers, the fraction that were returned.
> They pull against each other. For coronavirus origin, 637 papers are judged relevant. BM25's top ten has nine of them: precision 0.9, but recall only 1.4 percent. Its top thousand finds 255: recall 40 percent, but precision down to about a quarter. Return more, and recall can only rise, while precision usually falls.
> A ranked list is read from the top, so measure it at a cutoff. Precision at ten counts the relevant papers in the top ten: what people actually see. Recall at a hundred asks what share of all the relevant papers made the top hundred: what a reranker gets to see.

### 9. Order and grades

> Precision at ten ignores order: a relevant paper counts the same in first place as in tenth. Two measures care about order.
> Reciprocal rank is one over the rank of the first relevant result: one if it's first, a half if it's second. Its average over all the topics is the mean reciprocal rank, MRR. It suits searches with one right answer.
> NDCG also uses the grades. Each paper's gain is its grade: two for relevant, one for partially relevant. Divide each gain by a discount that grows with the rank, the log base two of the rank plus one, and add them up. That's the discounted cumulative gain, or DCG. For BM25's top ten, it's 7.06.
> The best possible top ten, ten papers graded two, would score 9.09. Divide one by the other, and you get normalized DCG, NDCG at ten: 0.78, where one is perfect.

### 10. Measuring the pipeline

> Now score every stage over all fifty topics. Here each topic's full question is the query, the standard setup for this collection.
> BM25 doubles TF-IDF's NDCG at ten, from 0.30 to 0.61. Dense retrieval alone scores 0.47, below BM25, though four in ten of its top papers were never judged. Hybrid search beats both, at 0.68. And reranking its top hundred lifts it to 0.74. Recall at a hundred doesn't move, because the reranker only reorders. MRR barely moves either: with hundreds of relevant papers per topic, almost any decent ranking has one at or near the top.
> Those are offline numbers: fixed judgments, repeatable, and cheap to rerun after every change. But judges aren't your users. Online evaluation shows a change to a random share of real users, an A/B test, and compares what they do: what they click, how often they rephrase, how often they give up.
> Clicks are biased, though. In one eye-tracking study, when the top two results were swapped, people still clicked the first one more, even when the second was more relevant. And a change that wins offline can still lose with users. So teams use both: offline tests to choose what's worth trying, online tests to decide what ships.

### 11. The answer

> So how do ten good papers come back from 171 thousand, in two milliseconds?
> Before any search, an analyzer turns every paper into terms, and an inverted index lists the papers for each term. A query reads a few posting lists, never the whole collection, and BM25 ranks the matches. To do better, add stages: vectors for the same meaning in other words, and a reranker for the top hundred, each costing more time.
> And relevance judgments, with measures like NDCG, tell you in numbers whether a change helped. Nine of that first top ten were relevant.
> The same pipeline now feeds language models. Retrieval-augmented generation searches first, and puts what it finds into the model's context.
